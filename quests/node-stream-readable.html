Node streams — Readable and Writable

Two halves of one wire. The same four branches on each side, the same buffer and mark, and one signal running in each direction.

A Readable divides cleanly into four branches. Three of them are surface you touch — state you read, methods you call, events you subscribe to. The fourth is different in kind: protocols is not API the class invented, it is a pair of contracts the class satisfies, and it is where the interesting claims live.

Every leaf below is clickable. This is the whole interface on one screen.

The same object, seen by role

The tree tells you what is on the object. It does not tell you which half of the contract each part serves, and that omission is behind the most common confusion about streams: reading read() and push() as two halves of one API. They are two halves of two different APIs that happen to meet at the same buffer.

Note the arrow directions. push() points into the core and belongs to whoever implemented the stream. The three surfaces point out of it and belong to whoever uses it. The tree draws them as neighbours on one branch; the roles lens shows they never meet except at the queue.

Why the fourth branch is not like the other three

State, methods and events are things Readable defines. Protocols are things Readable conforms to, and each was invented elsewhere for someone else. EventEmitter arrives by inheritance — Readable extends Stream, which extends EventEmitter, so on(), once() and the rest are not stream API at all. AsyncIterable arrives by implementing one well-known symbol.

That distinction is the reason the whole "is it just an async iterator?" question is answerable. The iterator is one of two protocols on the protocols branch, and the protocols branch is one of four. Three-quarters of the object is invisible from inside a for await loop.

You have been using these all along

Readable is rarely constructed directly. It is the shape returned by most of Node's I/O, which is why learning it once pays out everywhere.

Why null is not "false, but earlier"


A stream at null has never been asked for anything, so nothing is being pulled from the source and nothing can be lost. Attaching a 'data' handler flips it to true and starts the machine. The classic bug — attach the handler a tick late, lose the opening chunks — is therefore not really about timing. It is that observation is the trigger. At false the machine has already started and been told to hold, which is a different situation: bytes are arriving and piling up in memory.

The three consumption surfaces, in code


Same file, same buffer, same state machine. Only the arrival mechanism differs.



three surfaces onto one stream



The 'readable' idiom deserves its own look, because it is the only surface where you control both when you read and how much. It is the shape every framing parser ends up in.



the 'readable' plus read() loop



pipe() is backpressure wiring, not a shortcut


pipe() looks like a convenience for "send everything over there". What it actually does is subscribe to 'data', call write() on the destination, and — the whole point — pause() this stream whenever that write() returns false, resuming on the destination's 'drain'.





The gap in pipe(). Errors do not travel along it. If the source fails mid-transfer, the destination is never told and is left open — a leaked file handle or a truncated upload with no exception anywhere. pipeline() exists precisely to close that gap: it propagates the error and destroys every stream in the chain.



why pipeline replaced pipe for anything real

Ordering is the thing worth memorising


The two consumption idioms produce genuinely different event sequences. Reading them side by side is the fastest way to see why mixing them is a bug rather than a style choice.





Note the third 'readable' on the lower lane. It fires at EOF too, and the read() that follows returns null — that null is the end-of-file signal in this idiom, which is why the loop condition and the termination condition are the same expression.



Errors arrive differently on each surface


the same failure, three ways of catching it



Do not mix 'data' and 'readable' on one stream. They are not additive. Attaching 'readable' takes the stream out of flowing mode, so the 'data' handler you attached first can simply stop being called. Pick one idiom per stream.







The protocols branch is where Readable stops being a class with features and becomes an object that two independent contracts can both address. Neither contract was designed for streams.





The EventEmitter half


Because Readable extends Stream which extends EventEmitter, the entire emitter API is available and behaves exactly as it does anywhere else — including the ten-listener warning, which on a busy stream is a genuine leak signal rather than noise.



the inherited emitter surface



The AsyncIterable half


The contract is one method returning an object with next(). Readable implements it, which is the entire reason for await works. It is worth seeing that this really is a thin adapter and not a separate engine.

The inverse direction


Readable.from() goes the other way: hand it any iterable or async iterable and get a real stream back, with a buffer, a mark, and the full four-branch surface. This is the sharpest demonstration that the two things are not equivalent — the conversion adds machinery, and it only runs in one direction without loss.

Now the second diagram. Three prongs — buffer, events, async iterator — each with its own call, all converging on one consumer. The shape is right, and the thing it hides is that the prongs are not peers.





Read the legend colours again. The buffer is the storage. The events are notifications about that storage. The async iterator is a protocol implemented using both of the others — it subscribes to 'readable', and when woken it calls read(). Drawn flat, the three look like siblings. They are a substrate, a signal, and an adapter.



Cold and hot — where the buffer comes from


An async generator is cold. Its body does not run until next() is called, runs to the next yield, then stops dead. Nothing accumulates because nothing is produced unasked: consumption and production are the same act.



A Readable over a socket is hot. The kernel fills its receive queue on the network's schedule and libuv notifies the loop. The producing half runs whether or not a line of your code is waiting. Production and consumption are two independent processes — and every difference between a stream and an iterator is the machinery required to reconcile them.





Twelve pushes against three pulls. Those nine unconsumed chunks are the entire argument — they exist, they are somewhere, and "somewhere" is the buffer. A cold iterator has no such gap and therefore needs no queue, no fill level, no limit, and no way to tell a source to slow down.

State at rest. Between two next() calls an iterator exposes nothing — the protocol defines no surface beyond the promise you hold. Between two read() calls a Readable is a live object with a fill level, a flow mode and an end flag. You can ask a paused stream how much it is holding. There is no question you can ask a paused iterator.



Fan-out. Ten listeners on 'data' each receive every chunk. Two iterators over one stream split the chunks between them, because each next() pops the same queue. Observation and consumption are different relationships, and only one prong offers both.



Lifecycle visible to third parties. pipe() and pipeline() work by watching stream-level events on both ends. An iterator has no 'close' and no 'error' for an outside coordinator to subscribe to. You cannot build pipeline() from iterators without inventing an event layer — that is, without rebuilding the stream.



The iterator is a projection, not a peer




So the sentence to hold is not "a Readable has an iterator". It is: a Readable can be projected onto the iterator protocol, and the projection is lossy. You give up the fill level, the pause control, the fan-out and the third-party lifecycle. That is a fine trade for most consuming code, which is why for await is the right default. It is a bad trade the moment you need to know how full you are, hold a stream open without draining it, or hand the same bytes to two observers.



Where the projection leaks. Leaving a for await loop early — break, return, or a throw — destroys the stream by default. That is stream lifecycle reaching back through a protocol that has no vocabulary for it, and it is the most common way people lose data they meant to keep. The opt-out is readable.iterator().



the leak, and the opt-out







The state branch listed buffer as one leaf. It is the largest thing in the object, and it is a linked list of whole chunks rather than a byte array — a choice that explains nearly all of its observable behaviour.



Chunks queue in arrival order and leave in that order. Nothing is copied on the way in: the Buffer the producer handed over is the same object sitting in the queue. Copying happens only when your requested size does not line up with the chunk boundaries, at which point the queue slices or joins to give you exactly what you asked for.





The mark is advisory, and that word is load-bearing


highWaterMark does not cap the buffer. It changes what push() returns and whether the machine asks for more. A producer that ignores the false return can push a hundred megabytes into a 16 KiB stream and Node will queue every byte. A single chunk larger than the mark is likewise buffered whole — the queue never splits an arriving chunk to respect the limit.



Defaults differ by stream type. A generic stream.Readable defaults to 16 KiB. fs.createReadStream defaults to 64 KiB. In object mode the mark counts items, defaulting to 16. Quoting one number for "the" high-water mark is wrong.



what the return value of push() actually means



read(n) and the boundary problem


Because the queue holds whole chunks, read(n) has to reconcile your requested size against the sizes that happened to arrive. Smaller than the head chunk, it slices; larger, it joins across several; and if the queue cannot satisfy n at all it returns null and keeps everything rather than handing you a short read.



three requests against the same queue



That last case is the one to internalise. read(n) is all-or-nothing, so a framing parser can ask for exactly a header length and trust it gets the whole header or nothing — no partial-read bookkeeping. unshift() completes the picture: read a chunk, find you took too much, push the remainder back on the head.



Two accounting modes, one shape


Object mode changes what the queue counts, not how it behaves. Each push() queues one item, readableLength counts items, and read() ignores its size argument and returns exactly one. setEncoding() sits on the other side of the same problem: it decodes on the way out and holds partial multi-byte sequences across chunk boundaries, so a character is never split in half by an unlucky packet.



object mode, and the split-character problem







Production mechanics is the loop that keeps the buffer between empty and full while the producer and the consumer never speak to each other directly.



The decoupling is the point. A file descriptor knows nothing about your for await loop; your loop knows nothing about read syscalls. Between them the machine runs a demand loop — when the buffer has room it asks the producer for more, and when the buffer is full it stops asking. That is the whole mechanism, and backpressure is just the name for the second half of it.



Before the loop, the straight line. Four things happen between a byte existing at the source and your code holding it, and each is a separate step with its own timing.





Step four is the one that closes the line into a loop. Once the mark is involved, the chain stops being a pipeline and becomes a feedback system — which is what the next diagram draws.

Why all three surfaces behave identically here. The demand loop is driven by the buffer's fill level, not by which API drained it. Switch the surface mid-run and the fill bar keeps its shape. That is the concrete meaning of the second diagram: three prongs, one machine.







Writable divides the same four ways, and three of the four branches line up almost leaf for leaf with Readable. The fourth does not, and that mismatch turns out to be the most instructive thing on the whole diagram.





The empty slot is the thesis


Readable satisfies two contracts. Writable satisfies one. JavaScript standardised a pull protocol — AsyncIterable, driven by for await — and never standardised a push one. There is no language-level sink protocol, no Symbol.asyncSink, no syntax that means "feed this until it says stop".



The practical consequence is the single biggest asymmetry between the two halves. On the reading side the language hands you a loop that handles backpressure for free, because awaiting the next value is the flow control. On the writing side there is no such loop, so the flow control is a boolean return value you have to check by hand — and every unbounded-memory bug in Node streams traces back to somebody not checking it.



Not the same as having no options. WHATWG WritableStream does define a sink interface with a writer and a ready promise, and Node can bridge to it. But that is a platform API you opt into, not a language protocol the syntax knows about — which is why it does not appear on this branch.



The same object, seen by role


Reverse every arrow from the Readable roles diagram and you get this one. Your code is now the supply, the sink is the consumer, and the core sits between them holding exactly the same kind of queue.





Hold this next to the Readable roles diagram. There, three surfaces fanned out of the core because a consumer can be offered a choice of how to take delivery. Here there is one outlet, because the stream is not offering the sink a choice — it is handing chunks over on a fixed schedule, one at a time, and waiting for each to be acknowledged. That serialisation is a guarantee Writable makes and Readable never has to.



You have been writing to these all along





The stdout caveat. process.stdout and process.stderr are Writables with unusual timing: on POSIX, writes to files and TTYs are synchronous, while writes to pipes are asynchronous. So the same console.log behaves differently depending on whether output goes to a terminal or through a pipe into another program. They also cannot be closed the way an ordinary Writable can.



the same four branches, whatever the sink

ended is not finished


Calling end() flips writableEnded immediately and synchronously. At that instant the queue may still hold megabytes that have not been handed to the sink, the file descriptor may not have seen a single byte, and _final has not run. writableFinished is what turns true after all of that completes.



the two flags, and the gap between them



This is the truncated-output bug. Exiting the process, resolving a request, or reporting success on writableEnded — or worse, on the line after end() returns — publishes a file that is still being written. Wait for 'finish', the end() callback, or finished() from node:stream/promises.

write() has three arguments and people use one


The per-write callback is the most under-used part of the interface. It fires when that specific chunk has been handed to the sink and acknowledged — which is how you attribute an error to the exact write that caused it, rather than learning about it later from a stream-level 'error'.



the full signature, and what the callback means



What the callback does not promise. It fires when the chunk has been processed by _write — for a file stream, when the write syscall returned. It is not a durability guarantee. Bytes may still be sitting in the operating system's page cache; if you need them on the platter you need fsync, which streams do not do for you.



end() is two operations wearing one name

The drain threshold is not the mark. This is a real asymmetry with the reading side. A Readable resumes asking its producer as soon as the buffer drops below the mark. A Writable does not emit 'drain' until its queue is completely empty. So a writer that waits for 'drain' runs in bursts — fill to the mark, drain to zero, repeat — rather than riding the boundary.

One contract instead of two. Everything on this branch is inherited from EventEmitter, and the interesting content is what fills the gap the missing protocol leaves.

the inherited surface, and the promise helpers


crossing to the web streams world







One boolean. Everything about writing safely to a stream is contained in what you do with the value write() hands back, and the reason it matters is that ignoring it is not an error — it works, right up until it exhausts memory.



The contract


write() queues the chunk and then reports whether the queue is now at or over the mark. The chunk is accepted either way. There is no version of write() that refuses. The return value is a request that you stop calling, addressed to code that is free to ignore it — exactly like push() on the reading side, and advisory for exactly the same reason.





The grey marker is the part people get wrong. Crossing back under the mark changes nothing observable — no event, no callback. The stream stays silent until the queue is completely empty, and only then, and only if it owes you one, does it emit 'drain'.

The Readable buffer queues chunks. The Writable buffer queues write requests — and that difference is why cork(), _writev and the per-write callback can exist at all.



Each entry holds the chunk, the encoding it should be converted with, and the callback to invoke once it has been handed to the sink. A Readable's queue has nowhere to put a callback, because nobody is waiting on an individual chunk's fate.





cork and the batch


cork() tells the stream to stop forwarding and let the queue grow. uncork() releases it. The payoff arrives only if the sink implements _writev: instead of one call per chunk, the whole batch arrives as a single array, which for a socket means one syscall instead of twenty.





cork, uncork, and why the count matters



Corks nest and corks leak. writableCorked is a counter, not a flag: two cork() calls need two uncork() calls. Miss one and the stream silently accumulates everything you write to it forever, with no error and no 'drain', because nothing is being forwarded to notice. Pair them with process.nextTick or a try/finally.



Encodings and object mode


what happens to a string on the way in







Between write() returning and the bytes reaching the sink there is a chain with a guarantee attached: the sink sees chunks one at a time, in order, and never sees the next one until it has acknowledged the last.





The consequence worth carrying: write() returning true means queued, not delivered. Three synchronous writes all return before the sink has seen a single byte. The only way to know a specific chunk arrived is its own callback.



what ordering actually guarantees

four ways to wait, and the one to use



destroy() is not a polite close. It drops every queued chunk and every pending write callback on the floor. Use it to abandon a stream after a failure, not to end one normally — for that, end() is the method that flushes.







Laid side by side, the two halves are nearly a reflection. Reading the correspondences is the fastest way to make either side stick — and the four places the reflection breaks are the places worth memorising.

One sentence to carry both halves. A stream is a queue with a limit, plus a signal that fires when the limit is reached and another when it clears. Readable and Writable differ only in which side of that queue you are standing on — and therefore in which of the two signals you are obliged to obey.







Connect the two and you get the thing every real pipeline actually is: two independent queues, each with its own mark, with one signal crossing between them.





That chain is the entire point of the abstraction. A slow disk at the far end reaches back through two independent queues and two independent marks to throttle a socket read, and no line of application code participates. What pipe() contributes is only the middle link — subscribe, write, pause on false, resume on drain — and what pipeline() adds on top is the error path that pipe() never had.



the same chain, three ways to build it



Both halves in one object


A Duplex is not a stream that does both things — it is two of these machines in one object, with two queues, two marks and two lifecycles that do not know about each other. A socket can be finished writing while still reading.





two lifecycles in one object



Which is why a half-closed socket is not a bug. Calling socket.end() finishes the writable half and sends a FIN, while the readable half stays open and keeps delivering whatever the peer sends. 'finish' and 'end' fire independently, and 'close' waits for both.







A Duplex is not a stream that reads and writes. It is two complete stream machines sharing one object identity — two queues, two marks, two demand loops, two lifecycles — joined by nothing except the fact that they answer to the same variable.



That is why the map cannot be a single four-branch tree. The branches double. Every leaf you learned on the reading side is present, every leaf from the writing side is present, and only the protocols branch is genuinely shared.





The one shared thing matters more than it looks. Both halves emit on the same EventEmitter, so 'error' from either side arrives at the same listener and there is exactly one 'close' for the whole object. Everything else — the queues, the marks, the flow modes, the terminal events — is duplicated and independent.



Two machines, drawn honestly


The word "duplex" suggests data flowing through. It does not. Unless you are looking at a Transform, nothing connects what you write to what you read — a socket's two directions are as unrelated as two separate streams that happen to share a file descriptor.





two of everything, on one object







Every state getter you have met appears here, prefixed by which half it describes. The prefix is not decoration — it is the only thing telling you which of the two machines you are asking about.

The trap: writable and readable as booleans. Both exist on a Duplex and answer about different halves. socket.writable can be false while socket.readable is still true — that is a half-open connection, not a broken one. Code that checks one and infers the other is the single most common Duplex bug.

There is no _readAndWrite. The two hooks are called by two independent loops on their own schedules. If your Duplex needs the halves to coordinate — a request/response protocol, say — you hold that state yourself between the two hooks. Node will not do it for you.

waiting for the right thing



await finished(duplex) resolves when the stream is fully done — both halves. If you only care that your writes landed, wait on 'finish'; if you only care that the peer stopped, wait on 'end'. Using the wrong one on a long-lived socket is how a request handler hangs until the connection times out.







Half-open is the single behaviour that only exists because the halves are independent, and the one that surprises people most: a stream can be completely finished writing while still delivering data.



On a TCP socket it maps directly onto the protocol. Calling end() sends a FIN, which tells the peer I have nothing more to send. It says nothing about what the peer may still send you, and the readable half stays open until they send their own FIN.





allowHalfOpen


The allowHalfOpen option decides whether the two halves are allowed to end at different times. It defaults true for a raw Duplex and for net.Socket, and that default is why a socket you thought you closed can sit open for minutes.

the two settings, and what each costs you



This is why request/response over a socket works at all. An HTTP client sends its request and ends the writable half, then waits for the response on the readable half. With allowHalfOpen: false and no care, ending the request would tear down the path the response needs to arrive on.







Duplexes are the streams you touch most often without naming them. Every one of them is two machines sharing an object.

Duplex.from — the constructor shortcut. Hand it a source and a sink and it wires both halves for you, which is the quickest way to put a Duplex in a pipeline without writing a class.



building one without a subclass

A Transform is a Duplex where the two halves are connected — and connected by exactly one thing: a function you write. Everything else is inherited unchanged.



That single addition changes what the object is for. A Duplex is two conversations that happen to share a handle. A Transform is one conversation with a stage in the middle, which is why it is the only stream shape you can meaningfully put in the middle of a pipeline.





The most common wrong mental model. People picture a Transform as one buffer with a function over it. There are two, with two independent marks, and a Transform can be simultaneously refusing writes and starving its consumer — which happens whenever the hook is slow.

One function, three responsibilities: take a chunk, emit whatever it becomes, and say when you are done with it. The third is the one that gets forgotten.

There are two ways to emit, and they are not equivalent. cb(null, value) is shorthand for exactly one push; push() can be called any number of times, including none.

Async work belongs inside the hook, not around it. _transform is allowed to be slow — that is the point of the callback. What it must not do is call back before the work finishes, because the machine will then deliver the next chunk while the previous one is still in flight and the ordering guarantee is gone.



doing async work correctly







Two queues, two marks, one hook between them. Backpressure through a Transform is the interesting case because the stall can originate at either end and travels the same way regardless.



The chain is worth stating precisely. A slow consumer fills the readable queue; push() starts returning false; a well-written hook stops pushing and waits; the callback is delayed; the writable queue fills because nothing is being dequeued; write() returns false to whoever is upstream. Five links, no code in the middle.





Which is why ignoring push()'s return value inside a hook is worse than ignoring write()'s outside one. A hook that pushes regardless keeps the readable queue growing unbounded, and because the writable side is still being dequeued normally, the upstream producer never learns to slow down. The one place backpressure could have been applied is the one place it was skipped.



Watch it stall


Move the three rates apart. When the consumer is slowest the readable queue fills first; when the hook is slowest the writable queue fills first. In both cases the producer eventually gets the same answer.

A Transform has one more ending than the streams it inherits from, and it sits in a gap most people do not know exists: after the last chunk is transformed, but before the readable half is allowed to end.





That gap is what makes aggregating transforms possible at all. A hook that batches records pushes nothing during normal operation — it accumulates — and would have no way to emit the final partial batch if the readable side ended as soon as input stopped. _flush is that opportunity, and the stream will not emit 'end' until it calls back.



the aggregating shape, which needs both hooks



Where the leftovers go


Almost every real parser has state that outlives the last chunk: half a line, an unterminated record, a checksum still to append. Without a flush those are silently dropped, and the failure looks like data loss rather than a bug.



the classic line splitter, remainder and all



Forgetting _flush loses the tail. A file with no trailing newline loses its last line. A batcher loses its final partial batch. Nothing errors, the stream ends cleanly, and the output is quietly short — which is why it usually survives testing on well-formed input.

bytes in, objects out



The asymmetric-mode transform is the workhorse. A CSV parser takes a byte stream and emits row objects: writableObjectMode: false with a byte mark measured in kilobytes, readableObjectMode: true with a mark measured in rows. One highWaterMark for both would mean either 16 rows of buffering or 16 KiB of rows.



Ordering, and what it costs


Because _transform is called one chunk at a time and gated by its callback, a Transform is strictly sequential. That is a guarantee, and it is also a ceiling: a slow hook is a slow pipeline, and no amount of concurrency elsewhere helps.



when the sequential guarantee is the bottleneck







Where Transforms actually turn up, and the two shortcuts worth knowing.





PassThrough


PassThrough is a Transform whose hook pushes exactly what it received. It sounds pointless and is genuinely useful: it gives you a stream you can hand out before you know what will fill it, a tap you can attach listeners to in the middle of a pipeline, and a buffer with a mark you control.



the identity transform, and what it is for



compose


compose() welds several transforms welded into one, so a chain can be passed around and piped as a single stream.



a chain that behaves as one Duplex



Version caution. stream.compose() and Transform.from() arrived in Node 16 and 17 and remain flagged experimental. pipeline() covers most of the same ground and has been stable far longer.
